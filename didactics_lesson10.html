Section 1 · Foundations

Error vs Mistake

❌
Not All Wrong Answers Are the Same
The distinction between ERROR and MISTAKE is crucial in language teaching. They have different causes and require different responses from the teacher.
FeatureErrorMistake (Slip)
DefinitionSystematic deviation showing a gap in knowledgeTemporary lapse; learner knows the rule
CauseLack of knowledge or incomplete learningFatigue, stress, carelessness, time pressure
PatternRepeated, consistentRandom, inconsistent
Self-correctionLearner CANNOT self-correct (needs instruction)Learner CAN self-correct when pointed out
Example"She go to school" (doesn't know 3rd person -s)Saying "He go" once when tired (knows the rule)
TreatmentRequires teaching/remediationJust point out; learner will fix it

Error — Competence Problem

Definition: A systematic deviation that reveals a GAP in the learner's knowledge
Key feature: Learner CANNOT self-correct because they don't KNOW the rule
Example: A learner consistently says "She go" because they haven't learned 3rd person -s
Response: Requires explicit teaching and practice

Mistake (Slip) — Performance Problem

Definition: A temporary lapse; the learner KNOWS the rule but fails to apply it
Key feature: Learner CAN self-correct when given the chance
Example: A student says "He go" once when tired, but usually says "He goes" correctly
Response: Simply point out the mistake; learner will correct themselves
⚠️Exam Tip: ERROR = systematic, repeated, learner CANNOT self-correct (competence). MISTAKE = temporary, random, learner CAN self-correct (performance).
Section 2 · Sources of Errors

Where Do Errors Come From?

🔍
Understanding Error Sources Helps Teachers Respond
Errors come from different sources. The two main categories are INTERLINGUAL (L1 influence) and INTRALINGUAL (L2 developmental).
🌐
Interlingual Errors (L1 Transfer)
Interference from the native language
Click to expand

Definition: Errors caused by the learner's L1 (native language) influencing their L2.

Also called: Interference, Negative Transfer, L1 Transfer

Example: Arabic learners saying "He go" (Arabic doesn't mark 3rd person singular)

Example: French learners saying "I am here since two hours" (French structure influence)

🔄
Intralingual Errors (Developmental)
Errors from within the L2 system
Click to expand

Definition: Errors caused by the learner's developing L2 system, not L1 influence.

Types include:

• Overgeneralization: Applying a rule too widely ("goed" instead of "went")

• Ignorance of rule restrictions: Not knowing exceptions

• Incomplete application: Using rules partially

• False concepts hypothesized: Misunderstanding rules

📚
Induced Errors
Caused by teaching/materials
Click to expand

Definition: Errors caused by faulty teaching, misleading explanations, or confusing materials.

Example: A teacher over-simplifies a rule, leading students to apply it incorrectly.

Also includes: Confusing textbook presentations, wrong models from teachers.

Key Concept: Overgeneralization

Definition: Applying a rule TOO WIDELY to cases where it doesn't apply
Most common intralingual error type
Example: "goed" instead of "went" (applying -ed past tense rule to irregular verb)
Example: "childs" instead of "children" (applying -s plural rule)
💡Memory Tip: INTER-lingual = BETWEEN languages (L1 → L2). INTRA-lingual = WITHIN the L2 (developmental, overgeneralization).
Section 3 · Key Concepts

Interlanguage & Fossilization

🧠
The Learner's Developing Language System
Interlanguage is the learner's evolving personal grammar system — neither L1 nor L2, but a transitional system that changes over time.

Interlanguage (Selinker, 1972)

Theorist: Larry SELINKER (1972)
Definition: The learner's evolving personal system of rules — neither L1 nor L2
Key features: Systematic, dynamic (changes over time), shows learner's hypotheses about L2
Example: A learner consistently says "She go" — this reflects their current interlanguage grammar

Fossilization

Definition: When errors in interlanguage become PERMANENT despite exposure and instruction
Key feature: Errors "freeze" and resist correction
Example: An advanced learner still says "I am agree" even after years of English instruction
Causes: Lack of motivation, sufficient communication success, age, L1 entrenchment

Related Concepts

U-shaped development: Learner temporarily returns to errors after seeming progress
Backsliding: Reverting to earlier interlanguage forms under stress
Example: A learner uses "went" correctly, then later says "goed" as they learn more -ed verbs
⚠️Exam Trap: INTERLANGUAGE = the learner's EVOLVING system (Selinker). FOSSILIZATION = errors that become PERMANENT and resist correction.
Section 4 · Who Corrects?

Types of Error Correction

✏️
Who Should Correct Errors?
Error correction can come from different sources: the learner themselves, their peers, the whole class, or the teacher. Each has advantages.
TypeDescriptionAdvantages
Self-CorrectionStudent recognizes and corrects their own errorDevelops autonomy; deepest learning; builds awareness
Peer-CorrectionAnother student identifies and correctsCollaborative; keeps students engaged; reduces teacher talk
Class-CorrectionTeacher asks the whole class to identify/correctInvolves everyone; shared learning; non-threatening
Teacher-CorrectionTeacher directly intervenes to correctProvides accurate model; efficient; necessary for persistent errors

Best Practice: Correction Hierarchy

1. First: Give the learner a chance to SELF-CORRECT
2. Then: Ask PEERS if they can help
3. Then: Open to the WHOLE CLASS
4. Finally: TEACHER provides the correct form if needed
💡Why Self-Correction First? Research shows that learners learn best when they notice and correct their own errors. Self-correction promotes deeper processing and autonomy.
Section 5 · Corrective Feedback

Corrective Feedback Techniques

🔧
How Should Teachers Correct Errors?
There are many techniques for correcting errors, ranging from IMPLICIT (indirect) to EXPLICIT (direct). Each has different effects on learning.

Implicit Corrective Feedback — Indirect

RECAST: Teacher repeats the utterance CORRECTLY without explicitly pointing out the error
• Student: "Yesterday I go to school." → Teacher: "Oh, you WENT to school yesterday."
• Non-threatening; maintains flow; models correct form

CLARIFICATION REQUEST: Teacher indicates they didn't understand, prompting self-correction
• Student: "I buy a new car yesterday." → Teacher: "Sorry, you did what yesterday?"
• Gives learner chance to reformulate; promotes noticing

REPETITION: Teacher repeats the error with rising intonation to highlight it
• Student: "She go to school." → Teacher: "She GO?" (with emphasis)
• Draws attention to error without explicit correction

Explicit Corrective Feedback — Direct

EXPLICIT CORRECTION: Teacher directly provides the correct form
• Student: "I seed a bird." → Teacher: "Not 'seed'. We say 'SAW'. I saw a bird."
• Clear and unambiguous; good for persistent errors

METALINGUISTIC FEEDBACK: Teacher explains the RULE without giving the correct form
• Student: "She go to school." → Teacher: "Remember, with 'she' we need the third person -s."
• Promotes rule awareness; learner applies the rule themselves

ELICITATION: Teacher prompts the learner to produce the correct form
• Student: "She go..." → Teacher: "She...?" (pausing for student to complete)
• Pushes learners to actively retrieve the correct form

Other Useful Techniques

Non-verbal signals: Gestures, facial expressions, finger correction
Note-taking: Record errors for later remediation (delayed correction)
Reformulation: Teacher rephrases student's meaning in correct English
Echoing: Teacher repeats correct parts, pausing at the error
⚠️Exam Trap: RECAST = teacher repeats CORRECTLY (implicit). REPETITION = teacher repeats the ERROR with emphasis (implicit). EXPLICIT CORRECTION = teacher states what's wrong (explicit).
Section 6 · Timing & Approach

When & What to Correct

Not all errors need immediate correction. Effective teachers consider the timing, the type of activity, and the type of error before deciding how to respond.

Timing: Immediate vs Delayed Correction

IMMEDIATE: Correct during the activity (good for accuracy-focused work)
DELAYED: Wait until the activity ends (good for fluency-focused work)
Principle: Don't interrupt fluency activities; note errors for later
Activity TypeFocusCorrection Approach
Accuracy ActivitiesGrammar drills, controlled practiceImmediate correction; focus on form
Fluency ActivitiesFree speaking, discussions, role-playsDelayed correction; note errors for later

What Errors to Correct?

GLOBAL errors: Affect overall meaning/understanding — ALWAYS correct
LOCAL errors: Minor, don't affect understanding — may ignore/delay
FREQUENT errors: Correct if pattern shows systematic problem
STIGMATIZING errors: Correct to avoid social embarrassment

Views on Error Correction by Method

Audio-Lingual: Errors are "bad habits" — prevent and correct immediately
CLT: Errors are natural — tolerate during fluency work; focus on communication
Silent Way: Self-correction via teacher cues (Cuisenaire rods, gestures)
CLL: Non-threatening correction; teacher as "counselor"
💡Key Principle: During FLUENCY activities, note errors and address them AFTER the activity. Constant interruption kills motivation and fluency development.
