Section 1 · Foundations

Beyond Behaviourism — The Cognitive Revolution

🧠
Why Behaviourism Wasn't Enough
Behaviourism explained habit formation but couldn't explain how children produce sentences they've never heard before. The cognitive revolution opened the "black box" of the mind — showing that learning involves thinking, understanding, and creativity.

In this lesson, you'll master three mentalist theories that challenged Behaviourism: Cognitivism (Piaget), Innatism (Chomsky), and Constructivism (Piaget). These theories focus on what happens inside the learner's mind.

The Big Shift: From Behaviour to Mind

Behaviourism = Learning is habit formation through repetition
Cognitivism = Learning involves mental processes (thinking, memory, understanding)
Innatism = Humans are born with the ability to learn language (LAD)
Constructivism = Learners build knowledge through experience and discovery
TheoryKey FigureCore BeliefView of Learner
CognitivismPiagetMind processes informationActive processor
InnatismChomskyLanguage ability is innateBorn with LAD
ConstructivismPiagetKnowledge is constructedActive builder
📚Teaching Connection: These theories support inductive teaching (discovery learning), meaningful input, and student-centered approaches — the opposite of ALM's drill-based methods.

Section 3 · Cognitivism

Cognitivism — The Mind as Information Processor

Cognitivism — Key Principles

Learning involves mental processes: thinking, understanding, memory, problem-solving
Language learning is creative, not just imitation
Learners use mental abilities to discover rules and create new sentences
Learning is meaningful when connected to what is already known
The mind stores and retrieves information like a computer

Unlike Behaviourism, Cognitivism opens the "black box" of the mind. It explains how learners think about language, form mental representations, and use prior knowledge to understand new information.

Information Processing Theory

Input → Information enters through senses
Processing → Mind organizes, connects, and stores information
Output → Learner retrieves and uses stored knowledge

The mind is compared to a computer: it receives input, processes it, stores it in memory, and retrieves it when needed. Practice strengthens memory connections and makes retrieval faster.

💡Teaching Implication: Connect new material to what students already know. Use meaningful contexts, not isolated drills. Help students organize information into mental categories (schemas).

Classroom Applications of Cognitivism

Meaningful learning — Connect new content to prior knowledge
Discovery learning — Let students figure out rules inductively
Problem-solving tasks — Engage mental processes
Graphic organizers — Help organize information into schemas
Metacognition — Teach students to think about their own thinking
🧠Exam Insight: If an exam mentions "mental processes," "meaningful learning," "schema," "information processing," or "discovery learning" — the answer is Cognitivism.
Section 4 · Innatism

Innatism — We Are Born to Learn Language

Innatism (Nativism) — Key Principles

Humans are born with the natural ability to learn language
Language is NOT fully learned from the environment
The brain has a built-in Language Acquisition Device (LAD)
Children discover grammar rules even from imperfect input
All languages share Universal Grammar — deep structural similarities

Chomsky directly challenged Behaviourism. He argued that children can produce sentences they've never heard before — something habit formation cannot explain. This creativity proves that language ability is innate, not just learned from the environment.

Language Acquisition Device (LAD)

A mental organ specifically for language learning
Allows children to discover the grammar rules of any language
Explains why children learn language so quickly with imperfect input

Universal Grammar (UG)

All human languages share common underlying structures
Humans are wired to understand these structures
The LAD uses UG to figure out any specific language's rules
🔬Poverty of the Stimulus: Children hear incomplete, ungrammatical, and limited language input — yet they learn perfect grammar. This "poverty of the stimulus" proves language must be innate; the environment alone can't explain it.
ConceptDefinitionExample
CompetenceMental knowledge of language (the internal grammar)Knowing that "He goes" is correct
PerformanceActual language use in real situationsSometimes saying "He go" when tired
⚠️Exam Trap: Competence is what you KNOW (mental grammar). Performance is what you DO (actual speech). Errors in performance don't mean errors in competence — we make mistakes due to tiredness, nervousness, etc.
Section 5 · Constructivism

Constructivism — Building Knowledge Through Experience

Constructivism — Key Principles

Learners build knowledge through experience, not by being told
New knowledge is connected to what is already known (schemata)
Learning comes through doing and interaction
Students discover knowledge (inductive/discovery approach)
Teacher is a guide, not a knowledge-giver
Development happens before learning (readiness matters)

Piaget believed children are not passive receivers of information — they are active builders of knowledge. They construct understanding by reflecting on their experiences and connecting new information to existing mental frameworks (schemas).

Key Learning Processes

Schema = Mental framework for organizing knowledge
Assimilation = Adding new info to an existing schema
(e.g., Child knows "dog" → sees a new dog → adds it to "dog" schema)
Accommodation = Adjusting schema when new info doesn't fit
(e.g., Child sees a cat → realizes it's NOT a dog → creates new "cat" schema)
Equilibrium = Balance between what you know and experience
Disequilibrium = When new experience challenges existing schemas
💡The Learning Cycle: Equilibrium → New experience creates Disequilibrium → Assimilation or Accommodation → New Equilibrium (with expanded understanding)

Piaget identified four stages of cognitive development. Children move through these stages as they mature.

1

Sensorimotor Stage

Birth to 2 years
Learns through senses and physical actions. Trial and error learning. Develops object permanence. Egocentric thinking (can't see others' perspectives).
2

Preoperational Stage

2 to 7 years
Imagination and verbal skills develop. Symbolic thinking (using words/images). Still lacks logical thinking. Begins to understand others' perspectives.
3

Concrete Operational Stage

7 to 11 years
Logical thinking with concrete objects. Can do math operations. Conservation (quantity stays same despite shape change). Egocentrism fades.
4

Formal Operational Stage

11 to 16+ years
Abstract, logical, and scientific thinking. Understands hypotheses, justice, ethics. Can think about possibilities, not just reality. Group-oriented.

Section 7 · Listening Comprehension

🎙️ Listen — A Teacher Applies Mentalist Theories

Press ▶ to listen to a teacher explaining how she uses Cognitivism, Innatism, and Constructivism in her classroom. Identify which concepts she is describing.

A Cognitivist/Constructivist Teacher Explains Her Practice
~45 seconds · British English

Teacher: "I believe children come to my classroom with an innate capacity for language — my job is to provide rich, meaningful input and let their natural abilities do the work. That's why I surround them with stories, conversations, and authentic texts rather than drilling isolated grammar rules."

"When teaching new concepts, I always connect them to what students already know — their existing mental frameworks. If they have a 'schema' for animals, I build on that. Sometimes their old knowledge works perfectly — they just add the new word to what they already have. But sometimes, their existing understanding doesn't fit the new information, so they have to adjust their thinking."

"I rarely tell students the grammar rule directly. Instead, I show them lots of examples and let them discover the pattern themselves. This way, they're actively building their own understanding, not just memorizing what I said. And I always consider their developmental stage — some abstract concepts simply need to wait until they're ready."

"The key difference from behaviourism? I focus on what's happening inside their minds, not just their observable responses."

Interview Practice — Explain the Theories

Practice explaining the difference between Cognitivism and Constructivism in 60–80 words. This is a common interview question since both are associated with Piaget.

✍️Structure your answer:
1. Briefly define Cognitivism (focus on mental processes)
2. Briefly define Constructivism (focus on building knowledge)
3. Mention they're both from Piaget
4. State the key difference
0 words (aim for 60–80)
✅ Good practice!

Model Answer:
"Cognitivism focuses on how the mind processes information — thinking, memory, and understanding. Constructivism emphasizes that learners actively BUILD knowledge through experience, not by being told. Both theories come from Piaget's work, but they have different emphases. Cognitivism is about HOW the mind works (information processing); Constructivism is about HOW knowledge is created (through schema, assimilation, and accommodation). In class, Cognitivism means connecting to prior knowledge; Constructivism means letting students discover rules themselves."
