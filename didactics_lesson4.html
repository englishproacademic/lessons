Section 1 · Foundations

Two Pillars of Modern Language Teaching

🌉
From Individual Learning to Social Interaction
Piaget focused on how individuals construct knowledge. Vygotsky added a crucial dimension: learning happens through SOCIAL INTERACTION. Krashen then applied these ideas specifically to second language acquisition.

This lesson covers two highly tested theories: Social Constructivism (Vygotsky) and Krashen's Five Hypotheses. Together, they explain WHY communicative, interactive, low-anxiety classrooms produce better language learners.

Section 2 · Social Constructivism

Vygotsky — Learning is Social

👥

Lev Vygotsky

Russian · Social Constructivism
Tap to reveal...

Vygotsky believed that cognitive development happens through social interaction. Unlike Piaget (who focused on individual construction), Vygotsky emphasized that learning occurs first BETWEEN people, then WITHIN the individual.

Key Contribution

Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) and the concept of Scaffolding

Core Principles of Social Constructivism

Learning is social — happens through interaction with others
More Knowledgeable Other (MKO) — Teacher, peer, or expert who provides guidance
Language is a tool for thought — We think through language
Learning leads development — (opposite of Piaget: development before learning)
⚠️Exam Trap — Piaget vs Vygotsky:
• Piaget: Development happens BEFORE learning (readiness)
• Vygotsky: Learning LEADS development (with proper support)
Piaget (Constructivism)Vygotsky (Social Constructivism)

Development before learningLearning leads development
Focus on stagesFocus on social interaction (ZPD)
Teacher as guideTeacher as MKO (More Knowledgeable Other)
Section 3 · Key Concepts

ZPD & Scaffolding — The Heart of Vygotsky

Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)

The gap between what a learner can do alone vs. what they can do with help
Zone 1: What learner can do independently (too easy)
Zone 2 (ZPD): What learner can do WITH guidance (optimal learning zone)
Zone 3: Beyond current ability even with help (too hard)

Learning is most effective when instruction targets the ZPD — not too easy (boring), not too hard (frustrating), but just challenging enough with proper support.

💡Memory Tip: ZPD = "With help, I can do MORE than alone." The teacher's job is to work IN this zone.

Scaffolding

Temporary support provided by teacher or MKO
Support is adjusted to match learner's current level
Support is gradually removed as learner becomes capable
Goal: Learner becomes independent

Just like scaffolding on a building is removed once construction is complete, teaching scaffolding is removed once the learner can perform independently.

🧠Examples of Scaffolding:
• Sentence starters: "I think... because..."
• Graphic organizers
• Think-alouds
• Peer tutoring
• Breaking complex tasks into steps
🗣️
Classroom Application
ZPD in action:
• A student can't write a paragraph alone, but CAN with sentence starters and peer help
• Teacher identifies this gap → provides scaffolding → student eventually writes independently

Scaffolding examples:
• Modeling: Teacher demonstrates the task first
• Prompts: "What comes next? Remember the structure..."
• Collaborative learning: Pair work with more capable peer
• Gradual release: "I do → We do → You do"
Section 4 · Krashen's Theory

Krashen's Monitor Model — 5 Hypotheses

📡
The Most Influential Theory in Second Language Acquisition
Stephen Krashen proposed five interconnected hypotheses that explain how people acquire (not just learn) a second language. His ideas transformed language teaching and support communicative approaches.

Stephen Krashen (American linguist) developed the Monitor Model in the 1980s. His five hypotheses are among the most frequently tested SLA concepts in teaching exams.

The Five Hypotheses

1. Acquisition vs Learning — Two different systems for developing language
2. Monitor Hypothesis — Learning acts as editor/monitor for acquired language
3. Input Hypothesis (i+1) — Acquisition happens through comprehensible input
4. Affective Filter — Emotions can block or allow acquisition
5. Natural Order — Grammar is acquired in a predictable sequence
📚Teaching Connection: Krashen's theory is the foundation of the Natural Approach (Krashen & Terrell) and supports CLT, comprehension-based methods, and low-anxiety classrooms.
Section 5 · The Five Hypotheses

Krashen's Hypotheses — In Detail

Tap each hypothesis to expand. These are heavily tested — know each one thoroughly!

1
Acquisition vs Learning Hypothesis
Tap to expand...

Acquisition = Unconscious, natural process (like L1). Develops through meaningful interaction. Produces "true" competence.

Learning = Conscious knowledge of rules. Formal instruction. Knowing "about" the language.

Key insight: Only ACQUISITION leads to fluent, spontaneous language use. Learning can only monitor/edit.

Example

A child "acquires" English naturally. An adult studying grammar rules is "learning" — but might not speak fluently.

2
Monitor Hypothesis
Tap to expand...

Acquisition initiates and produces language.

Learning acts as a "monitor" — edits and corrects output.

The Monitor works only when: (1) There's enough TIME, (2) Focus is on FORM (not meaning), (3) The speaker KNOWS the rule.

Example

In casual conversation, you don't have time to "monitor." But in a written essay, you can use learned rules to edit.

3
Input Hypothesis (i+1)
Tap to expand...

Comprehensible Input = Language just beyond current level (i = current level, +1 = slightly above).

Acquisition happens when learners understand messages containing structures a little beyond their current competence.

Key insight: Speaking is a RESULT of acquisition, not its cause. Focus on INPUT (listening/reading), not forced output.

Example

A student at level 5 (i) needs input at level 6 (i+1) to progress — challenging but comprehensible.

4
Affective Filter Hypothesis
Tap to expand...

Affective factors: Anxiety, motivation, self-confidence.

High filter (stress, fear, low motivation) = BLOCKS acquisition. Input doesn't reach the LAD.

Low filter (relaxed, motivated, confident) = ALLOWS acquisition. Input gets through.

Example

An anxious student in a high-pressure classroom won't acquire language well, even with good input.

5
Natural Order Hypothesis
Tap to expand...

Grammatical structures are acquired in a predictable order — NOT the order taught in textbooks.

This order is similar across learners, regardless of L1 background or instruction order.

Key insight: Teaching grammar in a particular sequence doesn't change the natural acquisition order.

Example

Learners often acquire "-ing" before third person "-s" — even if taught "-s" first.

⚠️Exam Trap — i+1 vs ZPD:
• i+1 = Krashen's INPUT hypothesis (comprehensible input slightly above level)
• ZPD = Vygotsky's zone (what learner can do with help)
They're similar concepts from different theorists!

Section 7 · Listening Comprehension

🎙️ Listen — A Teacher Applies Social Constructivism & Krashen

Press ▶ to listen to a teacher explaining how she uses Vygotsky's and Krashen's theories in her classroom. Identify which concepts she is describing.

A Teacher Applies ZPD, Scaffolding & Krashen
~50 seconds · British English

Teacher: "My teaching is built on two key ideas. First, I believe learning happens best when students work together. I always pair struggling students with more capable peers who can guide them through tasks they couldn't do alone. That gap between what they can do independently and what they can achieve with help — that's where real learning happens."

"Second, I provide lots of comprehensible input — stories, conversations, and texts that are just slightly above their current level. Not too easy, not too hard — just challenging enough to push them forward."

"I also pay close attention to the emotional climate of my classroom. If students feel anxious or embarrassed, that blocks their learning. So I create a relaxed environment where mistakes are welcome and there's no pressure to speak before they're ready. Some students need a silent period where they just listen and absorb — and that's perfectly fine."

"When I teach new skills, I start by modeling, then we practice together, then they try independently. I provide support at first — sentence starters, examples, prompts — but I gradually remove that support as they become more capable. That's how they become independent learners."

Section 8 · Interview Preparation

Interview Practice — Explain the Theories

ℹ️Note: The written exam is 100% multiple choice (A, B, C, D, E). However, in the oral interview, you may be asked to explain concepts. This section prepares you for that.

Practice explaining how ZPD and i+1 are related but different in 60–80 words. This is a common comparison question.

✍️Structure your answer:
1. Define ZPD (Vygotsky) — the learning zone with help
2. Define i+1 (Krashen) — input slightly above level
3. State their similarity (both = optimal challenge level)
4. State their difference (ZPD = general learning; i+1 = language input specifically)
0 words (aim for 60–80)
✅ Good practice!

Model Answer:
"ZPD (Vygotsky) is the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with help from an MKO. i+1 (Krashen) is comprehensible input slightly above the learner's current language level. Both concepts identify an optimal zone where learning is most effective — not too easy, not too hard. However, ZPD applies to ALL learning through social interaction, while i+1 specifically addresses language INPUT. A teacher applies ZPD through scaffolding and peer support; they apply i+1 by providing stories and texts just beyond current comprehension."
