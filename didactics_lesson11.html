Section 1 · Foundations

What is Classroom Management?

🏫
Creating an Effective Learning Environment
Classroom management is about organizing student behaviors and interactions to create a productive learning environment. It's essential for effective teaching.

Definition

Classroom Management: Organizing student behaviors, interactions, and the learning environment to maximize learning opportunities
Goal: Engage all students actively while minimizing disruptions
Includes: Rules, routines, seating, timing, transitions, teacher positioning
📋
Rules & Routines
Establish expectations

Best practice: Set rules WITH students at the start of the year. This creates ownership and buy-in.

⏱️
Timing & Pace
Keep momentum

Key: Careful lesson planning keeps students engaged. Vary activities to maintain attention.

🪑
Seating Arrangement
Facilitate interaction

Options: Rows, horseshoe, clusters, pairs. Choose based on activity type and class size.

🚶
Teacher Positioning
Proximity & movement

Tip: Move around the room. Proximity helps prevent and address behavior issues non-verbally.

Preventing Disruptive Behavior

Careful lesson planning: Engaging activities reduce opportunities for disruption
Clear instructions: Use ICQs (Instruction Checking Questions) to ensure understanding
Established routines: Students know what to expect and what's expected
Positive classroom climate: Build rapport and respect
💡Key Principle: Prevention is better than cure. Good planning and clear expectations prevent most behavior problems before they start.
Section 2 · Teacher Roles

The Many Roles of a Teacher

👨‍🏫
Teachers Wear Many Hats
Effective teachers shift between different roles depending on the activity, the learners' needs, and the stage of the lesson.
🎮
Controller
Manages class & directs activities
Click to expand

Description: Teacher-centered; the teacher controls what happens and when.

When: Giving instructions, presenting new language, whole-class drilling

Example: "Open your books to page 45. Now repeat after me..."

📊
Assessor
Evaluates & provides feedback
Click to expand

Description: Evaluates student performance and provides corrective feedback.

When: Checking answers, correcting errors, grading work

Example: "Good job! Just remember, we say 'went' not 'goed'."

📂
Organizer
Sets up activities & groups
Click to expand

Description: Organizes students into groups/pairs and sets up activities.

When: Starting a new activity, forming groups, giving task instructions

Example: "Get into groups of four. Each group will have one of these cards..."

💡
Prompter
Encourages & nudges
Click to expand

Description: Encourages students and provides gentle prompts when they're stuck.

When: Students hesitate, need encouragement, or lose momentum

Example: "You're on the right track. What comes after 'I would like to...'?"

👥
Participant
Joins activities as partner
Click to expand

Description: Joins in activities alongside students as an equal participant.

When: Role-plays, discussions, games, to model or to balance numbers

Example: Teacher joins a role-play as a customer in a restaurant scenario

📚
Resource
Provides information & help
Click to expand

Description: Available to help and provide information when students need it.

When: Students working independently, need vocabulary, have questions

Example: Student asks "How do you say X in English?" — teacher provides the word

🔍
Monitor
Observes & notes
Click to expand

Description: Observes students during activities, noting errors for later feedback.

When: Pair/group work, free practice, fluency activities

Example: Teacher walks around during group work, noting common errors for delayed correction

🌱
Facilitator
Guides learning process
Click to expand

Description: Guides and supports learning without dominating; student-centered.

When: CLT activities, project work, learner-centered classrooms

Key: The facilitator role is central to Communicative Language Teaching

⚠️Exam Tip: FACILITATOR is the key role in CLT. CONTROLLER is teacher-centered. MONITOR observes during activities. Know the difference!
Section 3 · Interaction Patterns

Interaction Patterns in the Classroom

🔄
Who Talks to Whom?
Interaction patterns describe how communication flows in the classroom. Varying interaction patterns increases student talk time and engagement.
PatternCodeDescriptionWhen to Use
Teacher to StudentsT-SsTeacher speaks to whole classInstructions, presentations, whole-class drilling
Teacher to StudentT-STeacher speaks to one studentIndividual feedback, eliciting, nominating
Student to StudentS-SStudents speak to each other (pairs)Pair work, dialogues, information gaps
Students to StudentsSs-SsStudents speak in groupsGroup work, discussions, projects
Student to TeacherS-TStudent speaks to teacherQuestions, reporting back, presentations
Open PairsOPTwo students practice in front of classDemonstrating before closed pairs
MingleMStudents move around, talking to manySurveys, find someone who, information gathering

TTT vs STT

TTT (Teacher Talk Time): Time the teacher spends talking
STT (Student Talk Time): Time students spend talking
Goal: INCREASE STT and DECREASE TTT (students learn by using language)
Tip: Use pair/group work and avoid unnecessary teacher monologues

Common Interaction Techniques

Information Gap: Students have different information and must communicate to complete a task
Jigsaw: Students learn different parts, then teach each other
Role-play: Students act out scenarios (e.g., ordering food, job interview)
Mingle: Students move around the room talking to multiple classmates
💡Key Principle: The more students talk, the more they learn. Maximize STT through varied interaction patterns!
Section 4 · Seating Arrangements

Seating Arrangements

How you arrange desks affects interaction, classroom management, and learning. Different arrangements suit different activities and class sizes.

ArrangementBest ForAdvantagesDisadvantages
RowsLectures, tests, large classesEasy control, clear focus on teacher/boardLimited S-S interaction, teacher-centered
Horseshoe / U-shapeDiscussions, presentationsAll see each other, good for discussionsNeeds space, harder with large classes
Clusters / GroupsGroup work, projectsPromotes collaboration, easy group workCan increase noise, some may not see board
PairsPair work, dialoguesEasy pair work, good for conversationMay need rearranging for group work
CircleSmall classes, discussionsEqual participation, community feelNo clear focal point, impractical for writing

Choosing the Right Arrangement

Consider the activity: Group work needs clusters; presentations need horseshoe
Consider class size: Rows work better for large classes
Consider the room: Physical constraints may limit options
Be flexible: Rearrange desks for different activities
⚠️Exam Tip: HORSESHOE/U-SHAPE is best for discussions where all students need to see each other. ROWS are teacher-centered and limit S-S interaction.
Section 5 · Lesson Planning

Lesson Planning Essentials

📋
A Roadmap for Effective Teaching
A lesson plan is a detailed guide for instruction. It outlines objectives, materials, timing, and procedures to ensure effective, organized teaching.

Components of a Lesson Plan

Lesson title/topic: What the lesson is about
Objectives/Aims: What students will be able to do by the end
Time: Duration of the lesson and each stage
Materials: Textbook, handouts, audio, visuals, etc.
Procedures: Step-by-step activities with timing
Interaction patterns: T-S, S-S, group work, etc.
Anticipated problems: What might go wrong and solutions

Types of Objectives

Main aim: The primary goal of the lesson (e.g., "Students will be able to use present perfect for experiences")
Subsidiary aim: Secondary learning that happens alongside (e.g., vocabulary for travel)
Personal aim: Teacher's own development goal (e.g., "Reduce my TTT")
💡SMART Objectives: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. Good objectives state what students will DO, not what the teacher will teach.
Section 6 · Lesson Frameworks

Lesson Frameworks: PPP & ESA

Lesson frameworks provide a structure for organizing activities. The two most common are PPP (Presentation-Practice-Production) and ESA (Engage-Study-Activate).

PPP: Presentation → Practice → Production

1. PRESENTATION: Teacher introduces target language in context
• Teacher-centered; explicit teaching of grammar/vocabulary
• Example: Teacher explains present perfect with timeline on board

2. PRACTICE: Controlled activities to apply target language
• Drilling, gap-fills, controlled exercises
• Focus on ACCURACY; teacher monitors and corrects

3. PRODUCTION: Free practice; students create their own language
• Role-plays, discussions, writing tasks
• Focus on FLUENCY; students use language independently
ℹ️PPP Origins: Traditional framework, associated with structural approaches. Good for grammar lessons and beginner levels.

ESA: Engage → Study → Activate (Jeremy Harmer)

1. ENGAGE: Get students interested and emotionally engaged
• Games, pictures, questions, discussions about the topic
• Example: "Have you ever been to a foreign country?"

2. STUDY: Focus on language (grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation)
• Can be inductive or deductive; drilling; exercises
• Example: Students analyze how present perfect is formed

3. ACTIVATE: Students use language in communicative activities
• Role-plays, discussions, tasks (like Production in PPP)
• Focus on communication; errors noted for later
ℹ️ESA Flexibility: Unlike PPP, ESA stages can be reordered (e.g., Engage → Activate → Study → Activate for a more communicative approach).
FeaturePPPESA
CreatorTraditional / structuralJeremy Harmer
StagesPresentation → Practice → ProductionEngage → Study → Activate
OrderFixed, linearFlexible, can be reordered
FocusLanguage forms (grammar)Engagement first, then language
Best ForGrammar lessons, beginnersAll levels, communicative lessons
⚠️Exam Trap: ESA was developed by Jeremy HARMER. PPP stages are FIXED; ESA stages are FLEXIBLE and can be reordered.

Model Answer:
"PPP stands for Presentation, Practice, Production — a traditional lesson framework. In the Presentation stage, the teacher introduces new language in context. For example, the teacher explains present perfect using a timeline. In the Practice stage, students do controlled activities focusing on accuracy, such as gap-fill exercises. In the Production stage, students use the language freely in communicative activities like role-plays or discussions, focusing on fluency. The stages are fixed and follow this order."
